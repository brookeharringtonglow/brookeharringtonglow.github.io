Creatine gets a lot of attention. Here’s the grounded version: what it does, where the evidence is strongest, and how to think about a gummy supplement without expecting a shortcut.

01 / THE BASICS

What creatine does

Creatine is a compound your body makes and stores mostly in muscle. It helps supply quick energy during short, intense efforts such as lifting weights or repeated sprints. Creatine monohydrate is the form studied most often.

Research suggests it can support strength and high-intensity exercise performance for some people, especially alongside regular resistance training. Results vary; creatine does not replace training, balanced meals, or recovery.

Keep expectations realistic: Creatine is a routine supplement, not a “reverse aging” product or a substitute for medical care.

02 / THE STRONG-AGING ROUTINE

Build the habit around the basics

  • Do resistance exercise. Choose movements you can do safely and progress gradually.
  • Eat enough and include protein. Food supports training and recovery; needs differ from person to person.
  • Give recovery a place. Sleep and rest days are part of a sustainable routine.
  • Be consistent. A manageable plan you repeat is more useful than an extreme plan you abandon.

In studies of older adults, creatine has generally been evaluated alongside resistance training. A recent review found a modest strength benefit from adding creatine to training, while added effects on muscle mass and physical function remained uncertain.

04 / USING SUPPLEMENTS THOUGHTFULLY

Make an informed choice

Research protocols commonly use daily creatine monohydrate; loading is not the only approach. Follow the directions on the specific product label rather than combining products or taking extra servings to chase faster results.

Some people notice temporary water-weight gain or stomach discomfort. If you have kidney disease, are pregnant or nursing, take medication, or have a health condition, ask a healthcare professional before starting a supplement. Tell your clinician if you use creatine, since it can affect creatinine measurements used in some lab tests.
